\section{Introduction}

An LLM agent reads from many places. It reads retrieved documents and the output of the tools it calls. It reads
the descriptions of those tools, the files and skills it loads, and messages
from other agents. It also reads its own stored memory. All of
this arrives in one context window, and the model treats it as one body of
text. The model has no reliable way to tell which part of that text is
trustworthy and which part is not.

Two research communities study what goes wrong when untrustworthy content gets
in, and they use different words for it.

The security community studies content that an attacker placed there on
purpose. It names attacks such as indirect prompt injection, retrieval
poisoning~\cite{zou-2024-poisonedrag,zhong-2023-poisoning} and memory
backdoors, and it publishes defenses against
them~\cite{xiang-2024-certifiably,liu-2025-datasentinel,an-2025-ipiguard}.
Success means the attacker fails to achieve a goal.

The safety community studies content that became wrong without any
attacker. It names mechanisms such as lost-in-the-middle~\cite{liu-2023-lost} and
context rot. It also names knowledge conflict, where a retrieved passage
disagrees with what the model already
learned~\cite{xie-2023-adaptive}. It publishes fixes for
them~\cite{huang-2025-parammute,bi-2025-parameters,yuan-2024-discerning}.
Success means accuracy goes up.

The two communities describe the same underlying failure. A passage the model
should not trust enters the context window and changes the output. Only the
provenance differs. The model sees tokens either way.

The difference is not merely one of vocabulary. If it were, a reader could
translate between the two communities and lose nothing. They differ in
something load-bearing: what counts as a successful evaluation.

Researchers measure a fix for the accidental case on an average-case
benchmark, because nothing adapts to it. They measure a defense for the
adversarial case against a worst case, because something does. Those are not two styles
of the same test. A method can pass the first test convincingly and fail the second one
completely. Neither community's publishing process catches this, because each
reviews against its own convention.

So the field publishes, adopts, and cites a method without anyone asking
whether it survives the other community's validation regime.

That is a testable claim about the literature, not a complaint about it. We
test it by reading both literatures under one taxonomy and recovering every
experiment either side has run. The test finds a gap. We then run the
experiments that fall in it.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] How separate are the two literatures, and is there a region of the
  problem that neither has tested? We answer by coding \NCorpus{} papers from
  both sides under one taxonomy and recovering every (defense, attack) pair
  any of them reports (Section~\ref{sec:literatures}).
\item[RQ2] Does one community arrive at a problem the other has already
  named, and if so which one arrives second? We answer by dating the first
  paper from each side in every channel both of them work
  (Section~\ref{sec:parallel}).
\item[RQ3] When a defense built for one regime is actually run against
  the other, does it transfer? We answer by executing the pairs the literature
  skipped, from the authors' released code and weights
  (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}, \ref{sec:results}).
\item[RQ4] Where defenses fail, is the failure a property of individual
  methods or of a design choice they share? We answer with a payload built to
  separate the possible explanations rather than to maximize damage
  (Section~\ref{sec:distractor}).
\item[RQ5] Does that explanation survive an attacker who reads the defense and
  optimizes against it? We answer by building two such attackers against the
  one method that resisted (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).
\end{description}

The second and third questions are the ones a survey normally cannot answer.
They are what turns a coverage gap from an observation into evidence.

Others have surveyed both literatures before, each from inside itself. We
borrow from those surveys rather than compete with them. What is new here is
that one corpus, one taxonomy and one harness span both. That is what makes the
empty cell visible. A survey of one literature cannot report a gap that the
other literature defines, because the other literature is not in its corpus.
It also makes the cell fillable. The taxonomy that locates a missing
experiment also names the artifacts that would run it. To our knowledge
this is the first evaluation in which published defenses from one of these
communities meet published failure mechanisms from the other.

A negative result is easy to produce by accident. Four rules guard against
that, and we fixed all four before any run. A defense must first reproduce its
own published benefit in our harness. An attack must damage the undefended arm
in the same run. An effect below the detectability threshold counts as no
resolvable effect, not as no effect. And we commit every prediction to version
control first.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} states each rule in full, and
Section~\ref{sec:results2} reports three results that reverse without
them.

\paragraph{Scope.}
Three limits are worth stating before the contributions rather than after
them. Our retrieval-cell experiments use one item set per cell. They measure what a
generator does with poisoned context, not how hard a retriever is to poison. Most of them use one model family at one size. Our own model control shows
that a different model gives different outcomes. The numbers therefore hold
for models of that class, not in general. Every attack in the campaign, except the two in
Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}, knows nothing of the defense it meets. That bounds
attack strength from below. It gives conservative evidence when a defense
fails, and no evidence at all when one holds. Section~\ref{sec:threats} gives the full list.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The cell nobody fills (RQ1).} Across \NPureTrackPairs{}
  confirmed (defense, attack) pairs recovered from \NCorpus{} papers read in
  full, only \NCrossCommunityPairs{} (\PctCrossCommunity{}\%) cross between
  the two communities, and \textbf{a security defense has been evaluated
  against a safety failure mechanism exactly
  \NSecDefVsSafetyAttack{} time}, against \NSecDefSecAttack{} evaluations against
  security attacks (Section~\ref{sec:emptycell}). The
  supporting measurements are a joint taxonomy over channel, intent and
  consequence, of which \PctCubeEmpty{}\% of cells hold no paper from either
  side; a census of \NMechanismsBench{} named mechanisms and \NDefenses{}
  named defenses, coded by the regime each was actually validated
  against rather than the one it claims; and citation rates of
  \PctSecCitesSafety{}\% and \PctSafetyCitesSec{}\% in the two directions
  (Section~\ref{sec:literatures}).

\item \textbf{The same ground, found twice (RQ2).} \NSharedChannels{} channels have named
  mechanisms from both communities. The security literature arrives first in
  \NChannelsSecFirst{} of them, both arrive together in
  \NChannelsSameYear{}, and \textbf{the safety literature arrives
  first in none} (Section~\ref{sec:parallel}). The median lag where security
  leads is \MedianChannelLag{} year and the longest is \MaxChannelLag{}. A
  safety-regime paper naming a failure is therefore often renaming one the
  security literature named years earlier, in a body of work it cites
  \PctSafetyCitesSec{}\% of the time.

\item \textbf{A platform, and \NCampaignPairs{} pairs run on it (RQ3)} across
  \NCampaignImpl{} implementations and \NCampaignEpisodes{} scored episodes,
  each from its authors' released artifacts under an attack-works-first
  control and a fixed detectability threshold
  (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). \NOursCrossPairs{} of our \NOursPairs{}
  retrieval-cell pairs cross the community boundary, against
  \PctCrossCommunity{}\% of the literature's. At the dose most favorable to a
  defense, not one of 16 (defense, payload) pairs shows a resolvable benefit.

\item \textbf{One mechanism, not a list of failures (RQ4).} Context-reliance
  defenses --- methods whose intervention is a weighting between the
  context-conditioned and the memory-conditioned prediction --- relocate
  correctness rather than creating it. What they take from memory the attacker
  receives, and what they take from the attacker memory receives; in the three
  defenses that suppress memory, the two movements cancel to within 8
  percentage points. Neither
  direction moves anything toward the real document, because the axis these
  methods arbitrate on does not contain the distinction an attacker violates
  (Sections~\ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:distractor}).

\item \textbf{Two adaptive attacks on the one defense that resisted (RQ5).}
  ACD~\cite{kim-2024-adaptive} weights the two distributions by an entropy term computed from the
  retrieved context, which an attacker owns, so the attack has exactly one
  objective: minimize that term. A best-of-five search over sentence forms
  removes 29\% of its protection; a gradient search over token ids removes
  \textbf{58\%}. Both stay above the ceiling the rule itself implies, so the
  method degrades rather than collapses, and the price is now measured
  (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).

\item \textbf{Three controls the literature omits.} Each one decides how a
  result reads (Section~\ref{sec:results2}). Against a \textbf{no-retrieval
  baseline}, the flattest defense under attack turns out to be flat because
  it has stopped reading the document. On an \textbf{abstention axis}, a
  defense with no effect on accuracy is converting a third of its answers
  into refusals. Under a \textbf{model control}, changing the base model
  alone reverses outcomes.

\item \textbf{A runnable artifact.} Every attack and defense executes from its
  authors' released code at a pinned commit, verified unmodified; the
  harness, the payloads and the full census ship with it.
\end{itemize}
