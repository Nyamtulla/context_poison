\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Two research communities study one failure. An LLM agent reads content it
should not trust, and it cannot tell which content that is. The security
community calls the content an attack and builds defenses. The safety community calls it a mechanism and builds fixes.

We measured the distance between them over \NCorpus{} papers read in full,
of which \NAnalysed{} name a mechanism or a fix.
\PctSecCitesSafety{}\% of security-regime papers cite a safety-regime one. Only \NBothValidated{} of \NFixes{} fixes were
tested in both regimes. And \PctSameRegime{}\% of
tested (fix, mechanism) pairs stay inside a single regime. One direction of the
cross-testing matrix holds \NSecDefVsSafetyAttack{} pair in \NPureRegimePairs{}: a
security fix has been evaluated against a safety
mechanism exactly once.

We then ran that experiment. \NCampaignPairs{} pairs, all on the authors'
released artifacts. \PctCampCross{}\% of them cross the boundary the
literature does not.

In the first direction, fixes built for honest error meet attacks. One
poisoned passage in ten gives a fix its best chance, because nine honest
passages survive, and it is the attacks' own default. \NGridLowHelps{} of
\NGridLowPairs{} (fix, mechanism) pairs show a resolvable benefit there.
The finding is an explanation, not a verdict. Context-reliance fixes do not
fail because someone built them badly. They fail because of what they
arbitrate. Each one decides between trusting the context and trusting memory,
and that dial moves correctness around without creating any. Suppress memory
and the attacker receives what memory held. Suppress the attacker and memory
receives it back.

A payload that plants a third answer separates copying from remembering, so
we can add the two movements together. In the three fixes that suppress
memory they cancel to within 8 points. Neither direction moves anything
toward the real document, because the dial has no setting for it. The attack
is inside the context.

In the second direction, a fix built against an attacker meets honest
mistakes. RobustRAG's vote holds while wrong passages are few, which is the
setting its threat model assumes. When half the retrieved passages are
stale, it returns the outdated answer more often than no fix does, and
against a failure that plants nothing it has nothing to act on. The two
directions fail for opposite reasons: the safety fixes assume the document
is honest, and the security defense assumes wrong passages are few. Each
assumption holds in its own regime and fails in the other.

Two results bound the first direction's claim rather than soften it.

First, protection depends on the \emph{form} of the planted passage, not only
on whether an attacker is present. An evaluation built around optimized
payloads therefore overstates how well a system holds.

Second, one fix did resist. It loses \PctAcdRemovedGradient{}\% of that
protection to an
attacker who reads its rule and optimizes against the quantity it measures.

\paragraph{Future work.}

\textbf{Build a fix that covers both regimes.} Section~\ref{sec:neither}
states what such a fix cannot rely on: trusting the document by default, or
wrong passages being a minority. Signals that need neither assumption exist
in the retrieval setting, among them when a passage was written, whether it
was retrieved for the question asked, and whether independent sources agree.
The next experiments build a fix on such signals and test it in both
directions on the same items, against the same mechanisms
reported here and against an attacker who knows the fix.

\textbf{Extend the adaptive case to the rest of the family.} We tested the
one fix that resisted,
with two optimizers. A best-of-five search over sentence forms took
\PctAcdRemovedBestOf{}\% of
its protection. A gradient search over token ids took
\PctAcdRemovedGradient{}\%. Together they
settle that the fix degrades rather than collapses.
Every other context-reliance method in the campaign has had only the
non-adaptive treatment, which Section~\ref{sec:experiments} states is no
evidence of robustness at all. The same two optimizers transfer directly: each
of these methods exposes a scalar the attacker can address.

\textbf{Close the supply-chain and cross-modal gaps.} These channels have fewer
than one named fix per named mechanism and are the routes by which agents
accept third-party capability. They are the clearest place to build rather than
to measure.

\textbf{Separate the intervention-point finding from its confound.} Whether a
defense generalizes appears to follow where in the pipeline it intervenes. A
confound sits beside that finding. The ingestion defenses that generalize are
also the ones whose harnesses model a text-classification victim, and the
execution defenses we could not evaluate are the agent-shaped ones. Running all three intervention points
inside one agent harness would resolve it.

\textbf{Make the empty cell the default, not the exception.} The deepest
problem this paper documents is not any single fix. It is that who runs an experiment predicts its
outcome, and that nothing in current practice corrects for this. A shared harness would fix this. It should pair fixes with mechanisms that a
taxonomy selects, not mechanisms that either author selects. Transfer then
becomes a measured property rather than an assumed one. We release ours.
