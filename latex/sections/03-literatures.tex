\section{Two literatures, one problem}
\label{sec:literatures}

Comparing the two communities requires reading both under one scheme. We
built one corpus from both, coded every named mechanism and fix on the same
axes, and recovered every (fix, mechanism) pair either side reports.

\subsection{Corpus}

We began from \NSeeds{} seed papers found during scoping, queried the Semantic
Scholar Academic Graph and arXiv APIs with keyword clusters drawn from both
vocabularies, and snowballed forward and backward. The discovery pool reached
\NDiscovered{} papers. \NHopTwo{} of those are hop-2 records, discovered
before the snowball depth was capped at one hop and kept in the database but
outside the working pool, which leaves \NWorkingPool{}. The artifact ships the
\NQueries{} queries with their dates and hit counts.

left and what it passed to the next stage on the right.}

We follow PRISMA~\cite{page-2021-prisma} for identification, screening and
inclusion, and Figure~\ref{fig:prisma} reports the count at every stage. Dual
coding at this scale is not feasible for a small team, so screening ran as two
passes over title and abstract against written criteria. The first removed
\NAutoExcluded{} records. The second read the \NSurvived{} survivors and
carried \NFullText{} of them forward to full-text retrieval. A record was removed at the second pass for one of two reasons. Its title and abstract placed it
outside the scope we had written down, or no full text was obtainable from a
peer-reviewed venue or a recognized preprint server, which is the case for
withdrawn papers and for records whose only version was an uncredentialed
copy. Reading an uncredentialed copy would put a claim in the census that no
venue stands behind.

We then read the \NFullText{} retrieved papers in full, coded them against
the inclusion criteria and kept \NCorpus{}, each carrying \NColumns{} recorded
columns of which \NCodedFields{} are coded from the full text. A paper stays
if it contributes an attack, a failure mechanism, a defense or a fix.
Benchmarks, surveys, measurement studies and position papers stay even though
they propose no technique, because they are how the field is organized. That
pass also recovered techniques the first extraction had missed, so the
registry counts here are floors.

Papers that name no technique are why the screened set and the analyzed set
differ. \NNoTechnique{} of the \NCorpus{} name no mechanism and no fix:
\NNoTechSurvey{} surveys or systematizations, \NNoTechBench{} benchmarks and
evaluations, \NNoTechFramework{} framework or architecture papers and
\NNoTechPosition{} position papers. The remaining \NNoTechOther{} match none of
those descriptions by title; they are applications, empirical studies of
off-the-shelf methods and practitioner guides, and Section~\ref{sec:threats}
reports a recall pass over them. The census, the taxonomy and the pair matrix
are computed over the \NAnalysed{} papers that name a technique, and the
citation analysis over all \NCorpus{}. AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti-2024-agentdojo}
is the clearest case: it is the environment \NAgentPairs{} of our own pairs
run in, and it names neither an attack nor a defense, so it sits outside the
analyzed set.

\subsection{A joint taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We place every named mechanism and every named fix on three axes: the
\textbf{channel} it concerns, the \textbf{consequence} it produces or
prevents, and the \textbf{regime} of the paper that names it. Each axis value
is named by the work that first identified it as a distinct surface or a
distinct harm; we group the names rather than coin them. The axes span a grid
of \NChannelCats{} channels by \NConseqCats{} consequences by two regimes,
\NCubeCells{} cells in all, which we call the \textbf{cube}. A security attack
and a safety mechanism that enter by the same route and cause the same damage
fall in the same channel and consequence and differ only in regime, so the
cube shows where one community has named a problem and the other has not.

\paragraph{Channel.} The channel is the part of the context window at issue:
the route the content took in for an insertion mechanism, and the stream that
grew, shifted or was evicted for a degradation one. There are
\NChannelCats{}. \meth{direct-input} is content placed in the prompt by the user
or an upstream service~\cite{liu-2023-promptb}. \meth{RAG} is a passage a retriever selected from a
corpus~\cite{zhong-2023-poisoning,zou-2024-poisonedrag}. \meth{tool-output} is whatever a called tool returned~\cite{greshake-2023-not}.
\meth{tool-metadata} is the names, descriptions and schemas of the tools
themselves, which the model reads before it calls anything~\cite{shi-2025-prompt}. \meth{memory} is
what the agent stored from an earlier turn or session~\cite{chen-2024-agentpoison}. \meth{skill} is a file or
instruction set the agent loads to extend itself~\cite{schmotz-2025-agent}. \meth{multi-agent} is a
message from another agent~\cite{chen-2023-scalable}. \meth{cross-modal} is content that arrives as an
image, audio or document and is rendered into text~\cite{zhu-2024-unraveling}. \meth{supply-chain} is
content entering through a package, template or model the agent depends
on~\cite{yan-2023-backdooring}.

\paragraph{Consequence.} The consequence is what the agent then does wrong.
There are \NConseqCats{}. \meth{goal-hijack} is pursuing an objective it was not
given~\cite{greshake-2023-not}.
\meth{reasoning-corruption} is reaching a wrong conclusion while pursuing the
right goal~\cite{liu-2023-lost}. \meth{silent-corruption} is producing a wrong output with nothing
to signal it~\cite{zhong-2023-poisoning}. \meth{data-exfiltration} is information crossing a boundary it
should not~\cite{yu-2023-assessing}. \meth{persistence-backdoor} is corruption that survives into a
later session~\cite{yan-2023-backdooring}. \meth{availability-loss} is the agent not delivering its work: either it
fails to finish the task at all~\cite{xia-2026-diagnosing}, or it finishes
only after spending compute, money or quota out of proportion to
it~\cite{debi-2026-whispers,feiglin-2026-benchoverflow}.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} places both axes on the system they describe:
channels on the way in, consequences on the way out, and the three points
at which a fix can intervene between them.

\paragraph{Regime.} The regime is the one assumption that separates the two
vocabularies of Section~\ref{sec:twovocab}: a security paper assumes an
adversary and a safety paper assumes none. Every paper takes exactly one.

\paragraph{What the census holds.} The registry holds \NMechanisms{} named
mechanisms and \NFixes{} named fixes, \NDefSecurity{} of them from the
security literature and \NDefSafety{} from the safety literature. By
consequence, \meth{goal-hijack} accounts for \NMechGoalHijack{} of the
mechanisms (\PctMechGoalHijack{}\%), followed by \meth{reasoning-corruption}
at \NMechReasoning{} (\PctMechReasoning{}\%). By channel,
\meth{direct-input} (\NMechDirectInput{}) and \meth{tool-output}
(\NMechToolOutput{}) together hold close to three fifths.

\paragraph{What the cube shows.} Figure~\ref{fig:cube} places every
mechanism and every fix in its cell, one panel per regime.
\NCubeEmpty{} of the \NCubeCells{} cells (\PctCubeEmpty{}\%) hold no named
mechanism and no named fix from either literature. The cube counts what the
field has named, not which papers discuss a cell, so a survey that ranges over
six channels occupies none of them. Four further patterns are visible in the
figure. First, the two panels barely overlap. The security literature holds
\NMechSecurity{} of the \NMechanisms{} mechanisms and masses them in
\meth{goal-hijack}; the safety literature holds \NMechSafety{} and masses them
in \meth{reasoning-corruption} and \meth{silent-corruption}. The columns each
community works are nearly disjoint. Second, effort is concentrated rather than scarce. Four cells hold more than fifty
fixes each --- \meth{tool-output} $\times$ \meth{goal-hijack} at
\NDefCellToolOutGoalHijack{}, \meth{direct-input} $\times$
\meth{goal-hijack} at \NDefCellDirectInGoalHijack{}, \meth{RAG} $\times$
\meth{silent-corruption} at \NDefCellRagSilent{} and \meth{memory}
$\times$ \meth{reasoning-corruption} at \NDefCellMemReasoning{} --- and
between them they hold \PctTopFourDef{}\% of the \NFixes{} fixes in the
census.

Third, \NCellsAttackNoDef{} cells hold a named mechanism with no fix ever proposed
against it. \NCellsAttackNoDefSec{} of them are in the security panel,
\NCellsAttackNoDefBackdoor{} of those are \meth{persistence-backdoor}, and the
last is \meth{memory} $\times$ \meth{data-exfiltration} on the safety side.
These cells lack any proposed fix, which is a stronger gap than a cell
studied by one community only. Fourth, the totals column adds up each channel over all consequences.
\meth{multi-agent} and \meth{memory} carry several fixes per mechanism, while
\meth{supply-chain} has fewer fixes than mechanisms and \meth{cross-modal} and
\meth{skill} sit near parity. Supply-chain and skill are routes by which an
agent accepts capability from a third party, and they hold the fewest entries
in the corpus.

\paragraph{What the fixes were tested against.} Almost every fix was tested
in one regime only. \NDefenseFix{} were tested against an adversary, the
defenses of Section~\ref{sec:twovocab}, and \NRemedy{} against incidental
failure, the remedies. \NBothValidated{} (\PctBothValidated{}\%) were tested
against both and \NNoValidation{} carry no test we could identify. So
\PctOneRegime{}\% of published fixes carry an untested cross-regime question,
which is the gap this paper measures and then fills. The regime a fix was
tested in is coded separately from the literature it was published in, and
only \NDefCrossRegime{} fixes were ever tested in the other community's
regime. So the gap that Section~\ref{sec:emptycell} shows for tested pairs also
appears for individual methods. Even so, \NMechUncovered{} of \NMechanismsBench{} named mechanisms
(\PctMechUncovered{}\%) have no fix ever evaluated against them. A single
umbrella entry, indirect prompt injection, absorbs more tested pairs than any
other entry by a wide margin, while \NInjUncovered{} of \NInjVariants{} named
injection variants have none at all. The field tests heavily against a
category and lightly against its members.

\subsection{Regime versus venue}
\label{sec:venue}

We code regime and venue independently, and the field does not divide
itself by venue. Of the
\NRegimeSecNamedVenue{} security-regime papers that name a venue,
\NRegimeSecAtAIVenue{} published at an AI venue and \NRegimeSecAtSecVenue{}
at a security venue. Adversarial work on LLM agents goes to ACL, EMNLP,
NeurIPS and AAAI about as readily as to S\&P and CCS. Only one community crosses over. Of the \NRegimeSafetyNamedVenue{}
safety-regime papers that name a venue, \NRegimeSafetyAtSecVenue{}
published at a security venue. Security researchers submit to AI
conferences; the reverse does not happen in this corpus.

One limit on the reading. A venue is coded only where the record carries
one, so this covers \NRegimeSecNamedVenue{} of \NRegimeSecurity{}
security-regime papers and \NRegimeSafetyNamedVenue{} of
\NRegimeSafety{} on the safety side. The rest are preprints with no venue
recorded, and we draw no conclusion about where they will land. So the two bodies of work are not kept apart by where they publish. What
keeps them apart is what they cite.

\subsection{Cross-citation between the literatures}
\label{sec:citation}

\textbf{Cross-citation is low in both directions.} \PctSecCitesSafety{}\% of
security-regime papers (\NSecCitesSafety{} of \NAnalysedSec{}) cite a
safety-regime paper, and \PctSafetyCitesSec{}\% of safety-regime papers
(\NSafetyCitesSec{} of \NAnalysedSafety{}) cite a security one. Even this low rate overstates how much
each side uses the other's work, because of which papers receive the
cross-regime citations. \NNetworkCross{} of the \NNetworkEdges{} resolved
edges (\PctNetworkCross{}\%) cross regimes, and they concentrate on a
handful of papers either side can cite in an introduction without adopting
anything from it. The four most-cited bridges are
lost-in-the-middle~\cite{liu-2023-lost}, Greshake et
al.~\cite{greshake-2023-not}, PoisonedRAG~\cite{zou-2024-poisonedrag} and
Self-RAG~\cite{asai-2023-selfrag}. These papers name the problem. Citing one sets the scene; it does not show
that a method from the other community was used. Figure~\ref{fig:network} shows the shape, two
clusters joined by a narrow band.

\subsection{Recovered fix--mechanism pairs}
\label{sec:coverage}

Whether either community tests the other's work is a question about pairs,
so we recovered every pair the corpus reports: \NPairsComposite{} confirmed
pairs. A (fix, mechanism) pair means some paper evaluated that fix against
that mechanism. The registry's unit is the claim: a fix enters it when its
paper proposes it against a named mechanism, not when the paper demonstrates
that it works. We recovered pairs by four routes: reading the papers that propose fixes, a
citation-and-full-text recovery pass, resolving benchmark names to their
constituent attacks where the paper named them, and a reverse scan of the
papers that propose mechanisms. The last route matters because it is the only
one that can observe a fix \emph{losing} to a mechanism published after it.

The matrix is sparse in a specific way. \NDefMatchedFull{} of \NFixes{}
fixes (\PctDefMatched{}\%) appear in at least one pair, and \NDefOneFull{} of
those (\PctDefOneFull{}\%) appear in exactly one. The most cross-tested fix
appears in \NDefCeiling{} pairs, and \NDefFourPlus{} appear in four or more.
Of the pairs belonging to those \NDefFourPlus{}, \PctBreadthFromAttack{}\%
came from papers proposing a mechanism rather than from the fixes' own
evaluations, and for the broadest case \NBroadestFromAttack{} of its
\NBroadestPairs{} do. Breadth here is not evidence that a fix was evaluated
widely by its authors. It is evidence that the authors of mechanisms chose it
as a target.

An empty cell of the matrix does not always mean that nothing was tested. A
defense evaluated on AgentDojo without naming which injection it faced was
evaluated adversarially, but it is not a named pair, because expanding a
benchmark into its constituent attacks would manufacture coverage.
Section~\ref{sec:artifacts} separates the fixes the matrix leaves empty into
those tested against an unnamed adversary and those never tested against one. The next two subsections read the matrix two ways: by who reported each pair,
and by whether its two sides come from different regimes.

\subsection{Reporter bias in recovered pairs}
\label{sec:whoran}

For every pair we also record which side of the field reported it and what
outcome was reported. Figure~\ref{fig:whoreports} puts the two reporters
side by side. Papers proposing a fix report it winning in \NOutcomeDefWins{} of
\NOutcomeDefPaper{} pairs, with the remaining \NOutcomeDefIncon{}
reporting no result we could resolve. Papers proposing a mechanism report the
fix winning \NOutcomeAtkDefWins{} times in \NOutcomeAtkPaper{}: they report it
losing in \NOutcomeAtkDefLoses{} and reach no directional verdict in
\NOutcomeAtkIncon{}. No pair has been reported by both sides:
\NOutcomeBothSides{} of the \NOutcomePairs{} pairs carrying a reported
outcome carry an account from both kinds of paper. Neither number measures how often fixes work. Together they
show that the outcome a paper reports depends on which side wrote it. That
gap is why the rest of this paper re-runs the fixes rather than citing either side's
claim.


\subsection{The untested region}
\label{sec:emptycell}


The axes so far describe what each community studies. The question this paper
turns on is narrower: \emph{has either community ever tested its methods
against the other's problems?} Sort every pair into four boxes --- who built the fix, against whose
problem it was run --- over the \NPureRegimePairs{} pairs whose two sides both
sit in one regime. The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} shows these
boxes; its right panel, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:campaign}, adds our
campaign.

Nearly every pair sits on the diagonal.
Each community tests its own fixes against its own problems:
\NSecDefSecAttack{} security-on-security pairs and
\NSafetyDefSafetyAttack{} safety-on-safety. The two off-diagonal boxes are
the ones that would answer our question, and they are almost empty.
\NSecDefVsSafetyAttack{} security fix has ever been run against a
safety mechanism; \NSafetyDefVsSecAttack{} safety fixes have been
run against security mechanisms. That is \NCrossCommunityPairs{} pairs in
\NPureRegimePairs{}, \PctCrossCommunity{}\%. That near-empty off-diagonal is the untested region. It does not show that
these methods fail outside their own regime. It shows that this has almost
never been tested, although a deployed system does not sort its inputs into
``an attacker wrote this'' and ``this is merely stale''.

The absence of cross-community pairs does not show that such tests were never
considered; authors may have omitted them because they expected them to fail.
Our data cannot establish why a test was not run. Three observations make
deliberate omission unlikely. Cross-citation between
the regimes is low (Section~\ref{sec:citation}), although citation counts
measure acknowledgment rather than awareness. No reported pair carries an
outcome from both the paper proposing the fix and the paper proposing the
mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:whoran}). And \NDefClaimExceedsTest{} fixes state an
adversarial threat model yet report no adversarial evaluation, which suggests
that gaps in evaluation are not confined to tests across the boundary.

The single crossing pair is TRACE~\cite{hong-2026-trace}, a trajectory compressor for long-horizon
agents whose own evaluation is adversarial, which is what places it in the
security regime, and it is reported against context-length-induced
degradation, a safety mechanism. The paper's subject matter spans both
communities. One pair in \NPureRegimePairs{} does not change the overall
pattern. The regime boundary is ours, not the field's, but drawing it left no paper
out. We assign the regime label per paper, every paper takes one of
the two, and papers on the boundary take the regime their own evaluation
adopts. A paper that spans both communities therefore still sits among the
\NPureRegimePairs{} pairs; we set none aside.

\subsection{Division of labor, not duplication}
\label{sec:parallel}

If the two communities duplicated each other's work, their methods would act
at the same stage of the pipeline. We test this with the coded intervention
point of each fix rather than with method names. Security acts where the
text enters: \NDefPtIngestionSec{} of its fixes act at ingestion.
Safety acts where the text is used: \NDefPtReasoningSafety{} of its
fixes act at reasoning. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} draws that split
against the pipeline and Figure~\ref{fig:intervention} counts it. Methods
that intervene at different stages do not duplicate each other. The names agree with the coding. Inside each taxonomy cell we looked for a
security-regime name and a safety-regime name close enough to be the same
thing relabelled: among \NMechanisms{} named mechanisms there are
\NMechNameClash{} such collisions, and among \NFixes{} fixes there
is \NDefNameClash{}. That test can only catch relabelling, so it confirms
the division rather than establishing it.


The two sides name different halves of one causal chain. \NSharedCells{} of our
\NOccupiedCells{} occupied cells hold mechanisms from both regimes,
\NMechSharedSec{} security and \NMechSharedSafety{} safety. Read them side by
side, and the security entries are overwhelmingly ways of getting bad text in, while
the safety entries are reasons the model believes bad text once it is there. The
\meth{RAG} $\times$ \meth{silent-corruption} cell is the clearest case:
PoisonedRAG, BadRAG~\cite{xue-2024-badrag} and their relatives on one side; over-trust in conflicting
external evidence, lost-in-the-middle, and knowledge-conflict circuits on the
other. The security papers build the attacks, the safety papers explain why
the model is susceptible to them, and the two sets of papers cite each other
rarely. The consequences each side names show the same division. Goal-hijacking, an
attacker's objective, is \NGoalHijackSec{} security mechanisms to
\NGoalHijackSafety{} safety ones. Reasoning corruption, a degradation, runs
the other way: \NReasonSafety{} safety to \NReasonSec{} security. That last reading is ours, taken from the mechanism names and their papers
rather than from a coded field, and some entries do not fit it cleanly. A few
safety-regime entries name a delivery route rather than a susceptibility,
\meth{KV-cache contamination} and \meth{incidental prompt injection} among
them. The intervention points the subsection opens with are coded, and the
argument rests on them.

If the two sides were duplicating each other, the cost would be wasted
labor and the field would still end up holding two fixes that work.
Dividing the problem costs something else: it leaves a chain with a gap in
it. A reasoning-stage fix sits downstream of an adversary who already
controls its input, and no amount of better reasoning recovers a document
that was written to be believed. This is what lies behind the results of
Section~\ref{sec:results}, where methods
that shift trust toward the retrieved document help when the document is
honest and hurt when it is written by someone else.

Publication dates say the same thing from a second direction, and they say
which way the division runs. \NSharedChannels{} of our nine channels have
named mechanisms from both regimes, and we compare the first paper from
each side in each of them.
The security literature arrives first in \NChannelsSecFirst{} of
\NSharedChannels{}. Both arrive in the same year in \NChannelsSameYear{}. The
safety literature arrives first in \NChannelsSafetyFirst{}. The
median lag where security leads is \MedianChannelLag{} year and the longest is
\MaxChannelLag{}. The \meth{supply-chain} channel, left out here because it has no
safety-regime entry at all, is the extreme case: security reached it and
safety has not. So a safety-regime paper naming a failure in a channel is usually studying a channel a security paper reached \MedianChannelLag{} to \MaxChannelLag{} years
earlier, in a literature it rarely cites. The name-collision count above shows that the later paper is not renaming
the earlier one's mechanism. The dates show that work in these channels has
mostly gone ahead without reference to the earlier results
of the other community. Two limits on this reading. The dates are publication dates in our corpus, so
a channel that security reached first in work we did not include would move
the count. And the direction may partly reflect that security publishes
attacks, which are discrete and nameable, while the safety side
publishes degradations, which are noticed before they are named.

The fixes that keep bad text out and the fixes that reason better about
text already present are built by different people, published at different
venues and evaluated against different problems, and no single fix in the
census covers both stages. A system that wants coverage at ingestion and at
reasoning therefore has to compose fixes from both literatures, and the
census holds no paper that does. Whether such a composition would help is a
separate experiment, which this paper has started and not finished: every
result in Section~\ref{sec:results} measures one fix alone.

Answer to RQ1. No. The two communities divide the problem rather than
duplicate it. Security guards where text enters and safety improves how the
model reasons about text already inside, and since neither tests against the
other's failures, a system built from either side alone stays open at the
stage the other side covers.
