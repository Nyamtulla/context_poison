\section{Two literatures, one problem}
\label{sec:literatures}

Two communities work one problem and rarely cite each other, which raises
RQ1: are they doing each other's work twice?

\subsection{Corpus}

We began from \NSeeds{} seed papers found during scoping, queried the Semantic
Scholar Academic Graph and arXiv APIs with keyword clusters drawn from both
vocabularies, and snowballed forward and backward. The discovery pool reached
\NDiscovered{} papers. \NHopTwo{} of those are hop-2 records, discovered
before the snowball depth was capped at one hop and kept in the database but
outside the working pool, which leaves \NWorkingPool{}. The artifact ships the
\NQueries{} queries with their dates and hit counts.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig9_prisma}
\caption{Corpus construction. Each stage reports what it removed on the
left and what it passed to the next stage on the right.}
\label{fig:prisma}
\end{figure}

We follow PRISMA~\cite{page-2021-prisma} for identification, screening and
inclusion, and Figure~\ref{fig:prisma} reports the count at every stage. Dual
coding at this scale is not feasible for a small team, so screening ran as two
passes over title and abstract against written criteria. The first removed
\NAutoExcluded{} records. The second read the \NSurvived{} survivors and
carried \NFullText{} of them forward to full-text retrieval. A record left at
the second pass on either of two grounds. Its title and abstract placed it
outside the scope we had written down, or no full text was obtainable from a
peer-reviewed venue or a recognized preprint server, which is the case for
withdrawn papers and for records whose only version was an uncredentialed
copy. Reading an uncredentialed copy would put a claim in the census that no
venue stands behind.

We then read the \NFullText{} retrieved papers in full, coded them against
the inclusion criteria and kept \NCorpus{}, each carrying \NColumns{} recorded
columns of which \NCodedFields{} are coded from the full text. A paper stays
if it contributes an attack, a failure mechanism, a defense or a fix.
Benchmarks, surveys, measurement studies and position papers stay even though
they propose no technique, because they are how the field is organized. That
pass also recovered techniques the first extraction had missed, so the
registry counts here are floors.

Those papers with no technique are why screening and synthesis are different
sets. \NNoTechnique{} of the \NCorpus{} name no attack and no defense:
\NNoTechSurvey{} surveys or systematizations, \NNoTechBench{} benchmarks and
evaluations, \NNoTechFramework{} framework or architecture papers and
\NNoTechPosition{} position papers. The remaining \NNoTechOther{} match none of
those descriptions by title; they are applications, empirical studies of
off-the-shelf methods and practitioner guides, and Section~\ref{sec:threats}
reports a recall pass over them. The census, the taxonomy and the pair matrix
are computed over the \NAnalysed{} papers that name a technique, and the
citation analysis over all \NCorpus{}. AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti-2024-agentdojo}
is the clearest case: it is the environment \NAgentPairs{} of our own pairs
run in, and it names neither an attack nor a defense, so it sits outside the
analyzed set.

\subsection{A joint taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We place every named mechanism and every named fix on three axes. Each
axis value is named by the work that first identified it as a distinct
surface or a distinct harm; we group the names rather than coin them. The axes let the two
literatures land in the same space without forcing them into the same words. A
security attack and a safety mechanism that enter by the same route
and cause the same damage occupy the same cell. They differ only in regime. That is what makes our taxonomy a way of finding missing
experiments rather than a filing scheme.


\textbf{Channel} is the part of the context window at issue: the route the
content took in for an insertion mechanism, and the stream that grew, shifted
or was evicted for a degradation one. There are \NChannelCats{}. \meth{direct-input} is content placed in the prompt by the user
or an upstream service~\cite{liu-2023-promptb}. \meth{RAG} is a passage a retriever selected from a
corpus~\cite{zhong-2023-poisoning,zou-2024-poisonedrag}. \meth{tool-output} is whatever a called tool returned~\cite{greshake-2023-not}.
\meth{tool-metadata} is the names, descriptions and schemas of the tools
themselves, which the model reads before it calls anything~\cite{shi-2025-prompt}. \meth{memory} is
what the agent stored from an earlier turn or session~\cite{chen-2024-agentpoison}. \meth{skill} is a file or
instruction set the agent loads to extend itself~\cite{schmotz-2025-agent}. \meth{multi-agent} is a
message from another agent~\cite{chen-2023-scalable}. \meth{cross-modal} is content that arrives as an
image, audio or document and is rendered into text~\cite{zhu-2024-unraveling}. \meth{supply-chain} is
content entering through a package, template or model the agent depends
on~\cite{yan-2023-backdooring}.

\textbf{Regime}, defined in Section~\ref{sec:twovocab}, is whether the
paper assumes an adversary.

\textbf{Consequence} is what the agent then does wrong. There are \NConseqCats{}.
\meth{goal-hijack} is pursuing an objective it was not
given~\cite{greshake-2023-not}.
\meth{reasoning-corruption} is reaching a wrong conclusion while pursuing the
right goal~\cite{liu-2023-lost}. \meth{silent-corruption} is producing a wrong output with nothing
to signal it~\cite{zhong-2023-poisoning}. \meth{data-exfiltration} is information crossing a boundary it
should not~\cite{yu-2023-assessing}. \meth{persistence-backdoor} is corruption that survives into a
later session~\cite{yan-2023-backdooring}. \meth{availability-loss} is the agent not delivering its work: either it
fails to finish the task at all~\cite{xia-2026-diagnosing}, or it finishes
only after spending compute, money or quota out of proportion to
it~\cite{debi-2026-whispers,feiglin-2026-benchoverflow}.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} sets the three axes against the system they
describe.

\NCubeEmpty{} of the \NCubeCells{} cells (\PctCubeEmpty{}\%) hold
no named mechanism and no named fix from either literature. The cube counts
what the field has named, not which papers discuss a cell. A survey that
ranges over six channels therefore occupies none of them. Emptiness is not uniform,
and Figure~\ref{fig:cube} puts the named mechanisms beside the named fixes in
each cell. Three things are visible there that a count of papers hides.

The two panels barely overlap. The security literature holds \NMechSecurity{} of
the \NMechanisms{} mechanisms and masses them in \meth{goal-hijack}; the safety literature holds \NMechSafety{} and masses them in
\meth{reasoning-corruption} and \meth{silent-corruption}. The columns each
community works are nearly disjoint.

Effort is concentrated rather than scarce. Four cells hold more than fifty
fixes each --- \meth{tool-output} $\times$ \meth{goal-hijack} at
\NDefCellToolOutGoalHijack{}, \meth{direct-input} $\times$
\meth{goal-hijack} at \NDefCellDirectInGoalHijack{}, \meth{RAG} $\times$
\meth{silent-corruption} at \NDefCellRagSilent{} and \meth{memory}
$\times$ \meth{reasoning-corruption} at \NDefCellMemReasoning{} --- and
between them they hold \PctTopFourDef{}\% of the \NFixes{} fixes in the
census.

And \NCellsAttackNoDef{} cells hold a named mechanism with no fix ever
proposed against it. \NCellsAttackNoDefSec{} of them are in the security
panel, \NCellsAttackNoDefBackdoor{} of those are
\meth{persistence-backdoor}, and the last is \meth{memory} $\times$
\meth{data-exfiltration} on the safety side. These cells lack any proposed
fix, which is a stronger gap than a cell studied by one community only.

The totals column collapses the consequence axis and reports it.
\meth{multi-agent} and \meth{memory} carry several fixes
per mechanism, while \meth{supply-chain} has fewer fixes than mechanisms and
\meth{cross-modal} and \meth{skill} sit near parity. Supply-chain and skill are
routes by which an agent accepts capability from a third party, and they hold
the fewest entries in the corpus.


\textbf{Census.} The registry holds \NMechanisms{} named mechanisms and
\NFixes{} named fixes, \NDefSecurity{} of them from the security literature
and \NDefSafety{} from the safety literature. By consequence,
\meth{goal-hijack} accounts for \NMechGoalHijack{} of the mechanisms
(\PctMechGoalHijack{}\%), followed by \meth{reasoning-corruption} at
\NMechReasoning{} (\PctMechReasoning{}\%). By channel, \meth{direct-input}
(\NMechDirectInput{}) and \meth{tool-output} (\NMechToolOutput{}) together hold
close to three fifths.

Almost every fix was tested in one regime only. \NDefenseFix{} were tested
against an adversary, the defenses of Section~\ref{sec:twovocab}, and
\NRemedy{} against incidental failure, the remedies. \NBothValidated{}
(\PctBothValidated{}\%) were tested against both and \NNoValidation{} carry no
test we could identify. So \PctOneRegime{}\% of published fixes carry an
untested cross-regime question, which is the gap this paper measures and then
fills. The regime a fix was tested in is coded separately from the literature
it was published in, and only \NDefCrossRegime{} fixes were ever tested in
the other community's regime. So the empty cell of
Section~\ref{sec:emptycell}, which is a statement about tested pairs, has a
counterpart one level down in the methods themselves.

Even so, \NMechUncovered{} of \NMechanismsBench{} named
mechanisms (\PctMechUncovered{}\%) have no fix ever evaluated against them. A
single umbrella entry, indirect prompt injection, absorbs more tested pairs than any other entry by a wide
margin, while \NInjUncovered{} of \NInjVariants{} named injection variants have
none at all. The field tests heavily against a category and lightly against its
members.

\subsection{Regime versus venue}
\label{sec:venue}

We code regime and venue independently, and the field does not divide
itself by venue. Of the
\NRegimeSecNamedVenue{} security-regime papers that name a venue,
\NRegimeSecAtAIVenue{} published at an AI venue and \NRegimeSecAtSecVenue{}
at a security venue. Adversarial work on LLM agents goes to ACL, EMNLP,
NeurIPS and AAAI about as readily as to S\&P and CCS.

\textbf{The traffic runs one way.} Of the \NRegimeSafetyNamedVenue{}
safety-regime papers that name a venue, \NRegimeSafetyAtSecVenue{}
published at a security venue. Security researchers submit to AI
conferences; the reverse does not happen in this corpus.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig5_citation_network}

graph. Security papers are red circles
and safety papers are blue triangles, so the two regimes stay apart in a

\label{fig:network}

One limit on the reading. A venue is coded only where the record carries
one, so this covers \NRegimeSecNamedVenue{} of \NRegimeSecurity{}
security-regime papers and \NRegimeSafetyNamedVenue{} of
\NRegimeSafety{} on the safety side. The rest are preprints with no venue
recorded, and we draw no conclusion about where they will land.

So the two bodies of work are not kept apart by where they publish. What
keeps them apart is what they cite.

\subsection{Cross-citation between the literatures}
\label{sec:citation}

\textbf{Cross-citation is low in both directions.} \PctSecCitesSafety{}\% of
security-regime papers (\NSecCitesSafety{} of \NAnalysedSec{}) cite a
safety-regime paper, and \PctSafetyCitesSec{}\% of safety-regime papers
(\NSafetyCitesSec{} of \NAnalysedSafety{}) cite a security one.

What carries the cross-regime citations makes the rate look better
than the engagement is. \NNetworkCross{} of the \NNetworkEdges{} resolved
edges (\PctNetworkCross{}\%) cross regimes, and they concentrate on a
handful of papers either side can cite in an introduction without adopting
anything from it. The four most-cited bridges are
lost-in-the-middle~\cite{liu-2023-lost}, Greshake et
al.~\cite{greshake-2023-not}, PoisonedRAG~\cite{zou-2024-poisonedrag} and
Self-RAG~\cite{asai-2023-selfrag}. These are the papers that name the
problem, and a citation to one places a paper in a setting rather than
showing a method crossing the boundary. Figure~\ref{fig:network} shows the shape, two
clusters joined by a narrow band.

\subsection{Recovered defense--attack pairs}
\label{sec:coverage}

A (defense, attack) pair means some paper evaluated that defense against that
attack. The registry's unit is the claim: a method enters it when its paper
proposes it against a named failure mechanism, not when the paper
demonstrates that it works. A fix in a pair therefore means
proposed-against, and the distance between proposing and demonstrating is
part of what this section measures. We recovered pairs by four routes: reading defense papers, a
citation-and-full-text recovery pass, resolving benchmark names to their
constituent attacks where the paper named them, and a reverse scan of attack
papers. The last route matters because it is the only one that can observe a
defense \emph{losing} to an attack published after it. The final matrix holds
\NPairsComposite{} confirmed pairs.

\textbf{The matrix is sparse in a specific way.} \NDefMatchedFull{} of
\NFixes{} fixes (\PctDefMatched{}\%) appear in at least one pair, and
\NDefOneFull{} of those (\PctDefOneFull{}\%) appear in exactly one. The
most cross-tested fix appears in \NDefCeiling{} pairs, and \NDefFourPlus{}
appear in four or more. But of the pairs belonging to those \NDefFourPlus{},
\PctBreadthFromAttack{}\% were contributed by attack papers rather than by
the fixes' own evaluations; for the broadest case,
\NBroadestFromAttack{} of its \NBroadestPairs{} pairs come from attack
papers. Breadth here is not evidence a method was evaluated widely by its
authors. It is evidence that attackers chose it as a target.

The complement of the matrix does not mean ``never tested'', because part of
the gap reflects our instrument rather than the field. A defense evaluated on AgentDojo without naming which
injection it faced was evaluated adversarially; it is simply not a named pair,
because expanding a benchmark into its constituent attacks would manufacture
coverage. So we classified every one of the \NDefEvalAudited{} fixes in the
registry by what its own paper reports in its evaluation section, asking
the coarser question: was this method run against an adversary at all?
\NDefEvalAdversarial{} were (\PctDefEvalAdversarial{}\%):
\NDefEvalPair{} appear in a confirmed pair, \NDefEvalProse{} carry an
unambiguous adversarial signal inside their own evaluation section, and
\NDefEvalBenchmark{} were run on an adversarial benchmark without naming
the attack. \NDefEvalNoBaseline{} state that they ran no real
comparison at all. \NDefEvalNoAdversary{} were evaluated with real
baselines but never against an adversary, on capability and quality
benchmarks instead. For the remaining \NDefEvalUnclear{} the coded fields
do not say.

That first group is exactly the set the matrix matches, so the rest of this
audit partitions the \NFixUnpaired{} fixes the matrix leaves empty, and it
answers the question the empty space raises. \NFixUnpairedAttacked{} of them
met an adversary whose attack carries no name the literature reuses: they
were tested, and pairing on names cannot see it. \NDefEvalNoAdversary{} met
no adversary at all. The \NDefEvalNoBaseline{} without a real comparison and
the \NDefEvalUnclear{} that do not say are the remainder. The empty space is
therefore mostly the field and partly this instrument, in a ratio the audit
fixes rather than leaves open.

That \NDefEvalNoAdversary{} is two populations, and they read differently.
\NDefNoAdvSafety{} are safety-regime fixes, most of them against
\meth{silent-corruption} and \meth{reasoning-corruption}. These are not
untested methods: a fix for a stale or conflicting passage is demonstrated
by accuracy rising on cases where the context is wrong, and there is no
adversary to put in the experiment. Their behavior under attack is
unmeasured rather than unsound, and they are the population this paper's
campaign probes. The other \NDefNoAdvSecurity{} sit in the security
regime, and \NDefClaimsAdvNoAdv{} of them name an adversarial threat model
in their own evaluation coding while putting no adversary in the
experiment, most against \meth{goal-hijack}. That is a different failing
from the first group. It is a defense whose evaluation does not meet its
own stated threat model, and it is the one place in this audit where the
gap is the paper's rather than the field's convention. The classification is conservative by construction --- anything
ambiguous lands in that last group rather than in a positive one.

Every count above is a positive finding: something the paper says, not
something a pass failed to find. We report no number of the second kind, and
the reason generalizes. We built three automated passes over these papers,
and hand-checking each found the same asymmetry: what a pass flagged as
present was reliable, and what it flagged as absent was about half wrong. A pattern can confirm that something is present in a paper;
it cannot establish that something is absent, because absence of the phrasing
it knows is not absence of the thing. A claim that something was never
tested should state whether it means ``not done'' or ``not resolved''.

\subsection{Reporter bias in recovered pairs}
\label{sec:whoran}

For every pair we also record which side of the field reported it and what
outcome was reported. Figure~\ref{fig:whoreports} puts the two reporters
side by side. Defense papers report their defense winning in \NOutcomeDefWins{} of
\NOutcomeDefPaper{} pairs, with the remaining \NOutcomeDefIncon{}
reporting no result we could resolve. Attack papers report a defense
winning \NOutcomeAtkDefWins{} times in \NOutcomeAtkPaper{}: they report it
losing in \NOutcomeAtkDefLoses{} and reach no directional verdict in
\NOutcomeAtkIncon{}. No pair has been reported by both sides:
\NOutcomeBothSides{} of the \NOutcomePairs{} pairs carrying a reported
outcome carry an account from a defense paper and an attack paper alike.

Neither number measures how often defenses work. The pair of numbers
measures publication incentive, and the size of the gap is the reason the
rest of this paper re-runs the defenses rather than citing either side's
claim.

\subsection{The untested region}
\label{sec:emptycell}

The axes so far describe what each community studies. The question this paper
turns on is narrower: \emph{has either community ever tested its methods
against the other's problems?}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig6_transfer}
\caption{Who built the defense (rows) against whose problem it was run
(columns). Left, every confirmed pair in the literature; right, every pair
in this campaign. Shade is each cell's share of its own panel, so the two
panels are not on one colour scale and the printed count is the value. The
literature crosses between the communities in \NCrossCommunityPairs{} pairs
of \NPureRegimePairs{} (\PctCrossCommunity{}\%); one of its two
off-diagonal boxes holds a single pair. All four boxes are occupied on the
right.}
\label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

Sort every pair into four boxes --- who built the defense, against whose
problem it was run --- over the \NPureRegimePairs{} pairs whose two sides both
sit in one regime. The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} shows these
boxes; its right panel, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:campaign}, adds our
campaign.

Along the diagonal, nearly everything is there.
Each community tests its own defenses against its own problems:
\NSecDefSecAttack{} security-on-security pairs and
\NSafetyDefSafetyAttack{} safety-on-safety. The two off-diagonal boxes are
the ones that would answer our question, and they are almost empty.
\NSecDefVsSafetyAttack{} security defense has ever been run against a
safety failure mechanism; \NSafetyDefVsSecAttack{} safety methods have been
run against security attacks. That is \NCrossCommunityPairs{} pairs in
\NPureRegimePairs{}, \PctCrossCommunity{}\%.

That near-empty off-diagonal is the untested region. It does not show that
these methods fail outside their own regime. It shows that this has almost
never been tested, although a deployed system does not sort its inputs into
``an attacker wrote this'' and ``this is merely stale''.

The absence of cross-community pairs does not show that such tests were never
considered; authors may have omitted them because they expected them to fail.
Our data cannot establish why a test was not run. Three observations are
nonetheless hard to reconcile with deliberate omission. Cross-citation between
the regimes is low (Section~\ref{sec:citation}), although citation counts
measure acknowledgment rather than awareness. No reported pair carries an
outcome from both a defense paper and an attack paper
(Section~\ref{sec:whoran}). And \NDefClaimExceedsTest{} fixes state an
adversarial threat model yet report no adversarial evaluation, which suggests
that gaps in evaluation are not confined to tests across the boundary.

The single crossing pair is TRACE~\cite{hong-2026-trace}, a trajectory compressor for long-horizon
agents whose own evaluation is adversarial, which is what places it in the
security regime, and it is reported against context-length-induced
degradation, a safety mechanism. The paper's subject matter spans both
communities. One pair in \NPureRegimePairs{} does not change the overall
pattern.

The boundary could be ours rather than the field's, and it is not drawn by
exclusion. We assign the regime label per paper, every paper takes one of
the two, and papers on the boundary take the regime their own evaluation
adopts. A paper that spans both communities therefore still sits among the
\NPureRegimePairs{} pairs; we set none aside.

\centering

Publication order does not close the gap either. \NDefensePredates{} of
\NTimelinePairs{} pairs (\PctDefensePredates{}\%) have a defense that
predates the attack it was tested on, so defenses do meet attacks their
authors could not have designed against. But only \NPredatesAndCross{}

attack and meeting the other community's attack are not the same event.

\subsection{Division of labor, not duplication}
\label{sec:parallel}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig10_intervention}
\caption{Where each regime's fixes intervene. Security keeps the text out
and contains what it does; safety improves reasoning over text already
accepted. Points run in pipeline order. Safety carries a dotted fill and is
the lower bar of every pair, so the two separate in print without colour.}
\label{fig:intervention}
\end{figure}

If the two communities duplicated each other's work, their methods would act
at the same stage of the pipeline. We test this with the coded intervention
point of each fix rather than with method names. Security defends where the
text enters: \NDefPtIngestionSec{} of its fixes act at ingestion.
Safety defends where the text is used: \NDefPtReasoningSafety{} of its
fixes act at reasoning. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} draws that split
against the pipeline and Figure~\ref{fig:intervention} counts it. Methods
that intervene at different stages do not duplicate each other.

The names agree with the coding. Inside each taxonomy cell we looked for a
security-regime name and a safety-regime name close enough to be the same
thing relabelled: among \NMechanisms{} named mechanisms there are
\NMechNameClash{} such collisions, and among \NFixes{} fixes there
is \NDefNameClash{}. That test can only catch relabelling, so it confirms
the division rather than establishing it.

The two sides name different halves of one causal chain. \NSharedCells{} of our
\NOccupiedCells{} occupied cells hold mechanisms from both regimes,
\NMechSharedSec{} security and \NMechSharedSafety{} safety. Read them side by
side, and the security entries are overwhelmingly ways of getting bad text in, while
the safety entries are reasons the model believes bad text once it is there. The
\meth{RAG} $\times$ \meth{silent-corruption} cell is the clearest case:
PoisonedRAG, BadRAG~\cite{xue-2024-badrag} and their relatives on one side; over-trust in conflicting
external evidence, lost-in-the-middle, and knowledge-conflict circuits on the
other. The security papers build the attacks, the safety papers explain why
the model is susceptible to them, and the two sets of papers cite each other
rarely.

The consequences each side names show the same division. Goal-hijacking, an
attacker's objective, is \NGoalHijackSec{} security mechanisms to
\NGoalHijackSafety{} safety ones. Reasoning corruption, a degradation, runs
the other way: \NReasonSafety{} safety to \NReasonSec{} security.

That last reading is ours, taken from the mechanism names and their papers
rather than from a coded field, and it is not clean at the edges. A few
safety-regime entries name a delivery route rather than a susceptibility,
\meth{KV-cache contamination} and \meth{incidental prompt injection} among
them. The intervention points the subsection opens with are coded, and the
argument rests on them.

If the two sides were duplicating each other, the cost would be wasted
labor and the field would still end up holding two defenses that work.
Dividing the problem costs something else: it leaves a chain with a gap in
it. A reasoning-stage defense sits downstream of an adversary who already
controls its input, and no amount of better reasoning recovers a document
that was written to be believed. This is the mechanism behind the results of
Section~\ref{sec:results}, where methods
that shift trust toward the retrieved document help when the document is
honest and hurt when it is written by someone else.

Publication dates say the same thing from a second direction, and they say
which way the division runs. \NSharedChannels{} of our nine channels have
named mechanisms from both regimes, and we compare the first paper from
each side in each of them.
The security literature arrives first in \NChannelsSecFirst{} of
\NSharedChannels{}. Both arrive in the same year in \NChannelsSameYear{}. The
safety literature arrives first in \NChannelsSafetyFirst{}. The
median lag where security leads is \MedianChannelLag{} year and the longest is
\MaxChannelLag{}. The \meth{supply-chain} channel, excluded here for having no
safety-regime entry at all, is the limit case of the same pattern.

So a safety-regime paper naming a failure in a channel is usually working
ground a security paper reached \MedianChannelLag{} to \MaxChannelLag{} years
earlier, in a literature it rarely cites. The collision count above shows
that it is not renaming that paper's mechanism. It does show that work in
these channels has proceeded largely without reference to the earlier results
of the other community.

Two limits on this reading. The dates are publication dates in our corpus, so
a channel that security reached first in work we did not include would move
the count. And the direction may partly reflect that security publishes
attacks, which are discrete and nameable, while the safety side
publishes degradations, which are noticed before they are named.

The methods that keep bad text out and the methods that reason better about
text already present are built by different people, published at different
venues and evaluated against different problems, and no single method in the
census covers both stages. A system that wants coverage at ingestion and at
reasoning therefore has to compose methods from both literatures, and the
census holds no paper that does. Whether such a composition would help is a
separate experiment, which this paper has started and not finished: every
result in Section~\ref{sec:results} measures one method alone.

Answer to RQ1. No. The two communities divide the problem rather than
duplicate it. Security guards where text enters and safety improves how the
model reasons about text already inside, and since neither tests against the
other's failures, a system built from either side alone stays open at the
stage the other side covers.
