\section{Background and threat model} \label{sec:background}

This section defines the terms the rest of the paper uses. It defines context
poisoning, sets the two literatures' vocabularies side by side and chooses one
for statements that span both, and states the system and threat model the
experiments assume.

\subsection{Context poisoning}

Context poisoning is our term for three things happening together.
Content enters an agent's context window. The agent should not have trusted
it. The agent's behavior changes as a result. The definition says nothing
about who put the content there. That is deliberate: it lets both literatures
fit inside one term.

A poisoned agent's output becomes another agent's input~\cite{lee-2025-prompt}, and poisoned memory reactivates in a later session~\cite{dash-2026-untrusted}. The term is wider than prompt injection, which is one channel and one intent. It is wider than knowledge conflict, which is one consequence. It is narrower than ``LLM safety'', because the damage must arrive through the context
window. Two things fall outside it. Training-time poisoning and weight attacks
do not travel through the context. Jailbreaks in which the user is the
attacker have no third-party content at all, which is the line Greshake et al.\ draw
between direct and indirect prompt injection~\cite{greshake-2023-not}.

\subsection{One failure, two vocabularies} \label{sec:twovocab}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig8_vocab}
\caption{One vocabulary from two. The top row names the content that should
not be trusted and the bottom row the countermeasure, first in each
literature's own words and then in ours. Each literature builds its
countermeasure for its own content.}
\label{fig:vocab}
\end{figure}

That one failure has two names, because two literatures arrived at it
separately. Figure~\ref{fig:vocab} puts the two vocabularies side by side,
with the one we use beside them.
The slots are the same: the same channel, the same position in the context
window, the same downstream consequence. Only the names differ, and one
assumption behind them. We take that assumption from Lin et
al.~\cite{lin-2025-safety-security}. \meth{Security} work assumes an
adversary and asks whether a system resists ``intentional attacks \ldots\ in
the presence of adversarial actors''. \meth{Safety} work assumes none and
asks whether a system avoids ``unintended harmful outcomes \ldots\ despite
uncertainties in inputs, goals, training data, or deployment conditions''.
We sort each paper in our corpus by which of these two conditions it
addresses: a \textbf{security paper} assumes an adversary, and a
\textbf{safety paper} assumes none. Every paper is one or the other. We sort
papers and not their authors, because the same researchers write both kinds
and no strict line separates two groups of people. The kind of a paper is not
its \textbf{venue}, the conference or journal that published it. The two are
easy to mix up, because security papers often appear at a security venue, but
Section~\ref{sec:venue} shows that security papers appear at AI venues just
as often. We write \emph{security venue} or \emph{AI venue} for where a paper
appeared, and \meth{security} paper or \meth{safety} paper for what it
addresses.
An adversary re-optimizes against whatever method is in place; a stale
document does not. That difference is real, because a method tuned to the
average case has no reason to hold at the worst case. It is also why
security and safety papers judge a countermeasure differently. Security counts it a success
when the adversary fails and tests it against the worst case. Safety counts
it a success when accuracy rises and tests it on average-case benchmarks.
How much the difference matters to
the model is a separate question, since the model reads tokens either way and
nothing in the text marks who wrote it. Section~\ref{sec:regime} measures
it. When the bad text states a wrong answer, a passage an attacker wrote to win
does more damage than a real outdated one. When it only takes the place
of the right evidence, an attack and an honest error do the same damage.

Because the two literatures use different words for one failure, we choose
one set of words for this paper, shown in the last column of
Figure~\ref{fig:vocab}. We use \emph{cause} for anything that makes the context
stop supporting the right answer, and \emph{fix} for anything meant to keep
it supporting that answer, whatever either one's authors call it. A cause may
be content that enters the context, as every attack does, or a weakness of
the model that the context sets off, such as lost in the
middle~\cite{liu-2023-lost}. Neither literature's own words cover both:
security papers name the input, and safety papers name the weakness. We use
these two words wherever a statement spans both literatures: the corpus
as a whole, a pair whose two sides come from different literatures, and the
campaign. A statement about one literature alone may keep that literature's own
words from Figure~\ref{fig:vocab}. Security papers call the content an attack, a
vulnerability or a threat, and the countermeasure a defense, guardrail,
safeguard or protection. Safety papers have no single word for the content: they speak
of degradation, failure, conflict or hallucination, and often name the
failure itself, such as knowledge conflict or noisy context. They call the
countermeasure a mitigation, method, framework or strategy. Neither literature
uses \emph{fix} or \emph{cause} as its general term. The closest precedent for
\emph{cause} is Xu et al.'s survey of knowledge
conflicts~\cite{xu-2024-knowledge}, which lists both misinformation and
outdated information as causes of conflict between passages.

Each of these two words covers two kinds. A cause is an \emph{insertion} when content
arrives in the window that should not be believed, and a \emph{degradation}
when nothing arrives. In the second case the context stops working because it
grew too long, because the decisive passage sits in the wrong place, or
because compaction evicted the rule the agent needed. Accuracy falls with the
right document present and correctly retrieved. Every attack is an
insertion. \emph{Attack} is not a third kind beside insertion and
degradation. It names the \NAttack{} insertion
causes an adversary wrote, and the \NAccident{} that a retriever, a
tool or the agent's own memory delivered with nobody behind them. We do not split causes by whether an adversary wrote them, because that
split is the same as the split between security and safety papers. Every cause
named in a security paper has an adversary, and no cause named in a safety paper
does. Insertion and degradation, by contrast, occur in both literatures:
\NSecInsertion{} of the \NMechSecurity{} causes from security papers are
insertion and \NSecDegradation{} are degradation, while the causes from safety
papers split \NSafInsertion{} to \NSafDegradation{}.

Fixes also come in two kinds, set by what their own evaluation tested. A \emph{defense} met an
adversary there. A \emph{remedy} met only incidental failure. That is
\NDefenseFix{} defenses and \NRemedy{} remedies of \NFixes{}. The split
records what a method was tested against, not how well it works. A fix that
filters incoming text has nothing to act on against a degradation, because
nothing new enters.

Two more words name where an answer comes from. The \textbf{context} is the document, tool
output or message placed in front of the model. \textbf{Memory} is what the
model learned in training and holds in its weights, which the safety
literature calls \emph{parametric memory}. A large family of published
fixes works by shifting the model from the second to the first.

Many of the systems we study fill the context by searching. Given a
question, they search a large collection of documents and paste the closest
matches into the context before the model answers. The search step is the
\textbf{retriever}, and the arrangement as a whole is
\textbf{retrieval-augmented generation} (RAG). Poisoning such a system takes
two steps: getting a planted document into the collection and picked by the
retriever, and then getting the model to believe it once it is in the context.

\subsection{System and threat model}
\label{sec:securityregime}

Both literatures study the same system, and only one of them assumes an
attacker inside it. The system is an LLM agent: a model that, within one task, repeatedly reads
content it did not author, decides, and acts. Content from many sources shares one context window, with
no trustworthy marking of where any of it came from. And an action taken in a corrupted context cannot be undone by fixing
the context afterwards.

\textbf{The adversary.} The adversary controls the content of at least one channel. They do not
control the model weights, the decoding procedure, or the agent's code. This covers the realistic
deployment cases. An adversary can write a document that a retriever may
fetch~\cite{zou-2024-poisonedrag}. They can publish a tool whose description
the agent reads~\cite{shi-2025-prompt}. They can post content that a browsing
agent visits~\cite{greshake-2023-not} or contribute a skill file that the
agent loads~\cite{schmotz-2025-agent}. The adversary knows the defense and
may adapt the payload to it. Two of the attacks we run, corpus poisoning and
BadRAG, use one fixed passage for every question. The attack PoisonedRAG and the planted
false answers are written for each question they target, so they assume the
attacker can anticipate which questions will be asked.

\textbf{Where there is no adversary.} Without an attacker there is no threat
model. The content goes wrong through the pipeline's normal operation. A retrieved document is out of date. A passage is close to the topic but
does not answer the question~\cite{shi-2023-large}. Important text sits in
the middle of a long input~\cite{liu-2023-lost}. A retrieved fact disagrees
with what the model learned in training~\cite{xu-2024-knowledge}. These causes do not adapt, so this literature evaluates on
average-case benchmarks.
