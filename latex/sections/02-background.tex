\section{Background and threat model} \label{sec:background}

This section fixes the terms the rest of the paper uses. It defines the
failure we study, shows that two communities name it twice, states the
system and the adversary we assume, and ends with the vocabulary the later
sections depend on.

\subsection{Context poisoning}

Context poisoning is our term for three things happening together.
Content enters an agent's context window. The agent should not have trusted
it. The agent's behavior changes as a result. The definition says nothing
about who put the content there, and that silence is deliberate: it is what
lets both communities fit inside one term.

A poisoned agent's output becomes another agent's input~\cite{lee-2025-prompt}, and poisoned memory reactivates in a later session~\cite{dash-2026-untrusted}. The term is wider than prompt injection, which is one channel and one intent. It is wider than knowledge conflict, which is one consequence. It is narrower
than ``LLM safety'', because the damage must arrive through the context
window. Two things fall outside it. Training-time poisoning and weight attacks
do not travel through the context. Jailbreaks in which the user is the
attacker have no third-party content at all.

\subsection{One failure, two vocabularies} \label{sec:twovocab}

That one failure has two names, because two communities arrived at it
separately. Figure~\ref{fig:vocab} puts the two vocabularies side by side.
The slots are the same: the same channel, the same position in the context
window, the same downstream consequence. Only the names differ, and one
assumption behind them. The security community assumes an adversary who
re-optimizes against whatever method is in place. The safety community
assumes a stale document or a retrieval error, and neither of those adapts.
That difference is real, because a method tuned to the average case has no
reason to hold at the worst case. It is also smaller than it looks, because
the model reads tokens either way, and nothing in the text marks which hand
wrote it. Section~\ref{sec:regime} tests the second half with payloads that
assert the same false answer, in the same slots, at the same dose, and differ
only in whether a person wrote the sentence to be believed.

Two vocabularies for one failure force a choice of words, and this paper
makes it once. We use \emph{mechanism} for anything that puts untrusted
content into the context window, whether an attacker wrote that content or a
pipeline delivered it by accident. We use \emph{defense} for anything that is
meant to stop such content from changing the answer, whether its authors call
it a defense or a fix. Provenance is not discarded. It moves to a separate
\emph{intent} axis, where the later sections read it off when it matters. The
alternative was to put ``attack'' at the top level of the taxonomy in
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}, and that would have made the safety half
unregisterable, because an attack presupposes an attacker.

\subsection{System and threat model}
\label{sec:securityregime}

Both communities study the same system, and only one of them assumes an
attacker inside it. This subsection states the system first and then the
attacker. The subject is an LLM agent: a model that, within one task, repeatedly reads
content it did not author, decides, and acts. Reading happens through the nine
channels of Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}. Acting means emitting text to a user
or calling a tool whose effects leave the system.

Three properties separate this from a single-turn chatbot, and our taxonomy
keys on all three. Content from many sources shares one context window, with
no trustworthy marking of where any of it came from. The agent's own output
can become another agent's input, or its own input a turn later through
memory. And an action taken on corrupted context cannot be undone by fixing
the context afterwards.

\textbf{The adversary.} The adversary controls the content of at least one channel. They do not
control the model weights, the decoding procedure, or the agent's code. This
is the standing assumption of the security literature, and it covers the realistic
deployment cases. An adversary can write a document that a retriever may
fetch. They can publish a tool whose description the agent reads. They can
post content that a browsing agent visits, or contribute a skill file that the
agent loads.

The adversary knows the defense and may adapt the payload to it. That is
precisely why average-case evaluation does not transfer. The adversary does
not need to know the user's query: every attack we run is query-agnostic by
construction.

We exclude three things. Training-time poisoning and weight-level attacks do
not travel through the context window. A user attacking their own agent
involves no third-party content, so the trust boundary we study does not
exist. Denial of service through resource exhaustion sits in the taxonomy as
\meth{availability-loss}, alongside the non-adversarial ways an agent fails
to deliver its work. It holds the fewest named mechanisms of any
consequence, \NMechAvailLoss{} against \NDefAvailLoss{} defenses, and we
draw no strong conclusion from it.

\textbf{Where there is no adversary.} The other literature works without
one, so what follows is not a threat model and we do not call it that.
Content goes wrong through ordinary pipeline behavior.
A retrieved document is stale. A passage is irrelevant but topically close.
Important text sits in the middle of a long input. A retrieved fact
contradicts what the model learned in training. The safety literature assumes this condition and evaluates on average-case benchmarks. That is the right
choice when nothing adapts.

\subsection{Terminology} \label{sec:terms}

The subsection above describes two conditions: one with an adversary and
one without. The paper needs a word for that distinction, and a second word
that is easy to confuse with it.

A \emph{regime} is the condition a piece of work addresses, and every paper
in our corpus takes one of two. We follow Lin et
al.~\cite{lin-2025-safety-security}. \meth{Security} work assumes an
adversary and asks whether a system resists ``intentional attacks
\ldots\ in the presence of adversarial actors''. \meth{Safety} work
assumes none and asks whether a system avoids ``unintended harmful outcomes
\ldots\ despite uncertainties in inputs, goals, training data, or
deployment conditions''.

A \emph{venue} is where a paper was published. It is not the same cut as
the regime, which Section~\ref{sec:venue} measures: adversarial work
appears at AI conferences as often as at security ones.

The word \emph{security} names a value of both, so we write
\emph{security venue} or \emph{AI venue} when we mean where a paper
appeared, and plain \meth{security} or \meth{safety} when we mean what it
addresses. A \emph{threat model}, the subject of the subsection above, is
narrower than either: it names one adversary, and only the security regime
has one.

\textbf{Context and memory.} A model answers from one of two sources. The
first is the \emph{context}: the document, tool output or message placed in
front of it. The second is what the model learned in training and holds in its
weights. The safety literature calls that \emph{parametric memory}. We use the
shorter \emph{memory} for it throughout, and every result table reports it as
\texttt{pm\_acc}. The distinction matters beyond bookkeeping, because a large
family of published defenses intervenes on exactly it;
Section~\ref{sec:whatweran} says which methods and
Section~\ref{sec:results} says what turning that dial achieves.
