\section{Threat model and system model} \label{sec:threat}

A security paper states its threat model before it taxonomizes. This paper
spans two literatures, only one of which assumes an adversary at all, so we
state the threat model and then the regime the other literature works in.

\textbf{Three words, kept apart.} A \emph{threat model} specifies an
adversary: their goal, what they control, what they know. A \emph{regime} is
which of two conditions a piece of work addresses, and we take the division
from Lin et al.~\cite{lin-2025-safety-security}: \meth{security} is
resilience against ``intentional attacks \ldots\ in the presence of
adversarial actors'', built on an asset, an adversary and a vulnerability;
\meth{safety} is avoiding ``unintended harmful outcomes \ldots\ despite
uncertainties in inputs, goals, training data, or deployment conditions'', in
non-adversarial environments. A \emph{venue} is where a paper published.

Regime and venue are coded independently and they are not the same cut, which
Section~\ref{sec:venue} measures. Because the word \meth{security} names a
value of both, we always write \emph{security venue} or \emph{AI venue} when
we mean the venue, and \meth{security} or \meth{safety} unqualified when we
mean the regime.

\subsection{System model}

The subject is an LLM agent: a model that, within one task, repeatedly reads
content it did not author, decides, and acts. Reading happens through the nine
channels of Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}. Acting means emitting text to a user
or calling a tool whose effects leave the system.

Three properties separate this from a single-turn chatbot, and the taxonomy
keys on all three. Content from many sources shares one context window, with
no trustworthy marking of where any of it came from. The agent's own output
can become another agent's input, or its own input a turn later through
memory. And an action taken on corrupted context cannot be undone by fixing
the context afterwards.

\subsection{The security regime, and its threat model}

The adversary controls the content of at least one channel. They do not
control the model weights, the decoding procedure, or the agent's code. This
is the standing assumption of the security literature, and it covers the realistic
deployment cases. An adversary can write a document that a retriever may
fetch. They can publish a tool whose description the agent reads. They can
post content that a browsing agent visits, or contribute a skill file that the
agent loads.

The adversary knows the defense and may adapt the payload to it. That is
precisely why average-case evaluation does not transfer. The adversary does
not need to know the user's query: every attack we run is query-agnostic by
construction.

We exclude three things. Training-time poisoning and weight-level attacks do
not travel through the context window. A user attacking their own agent
involves no third-party content, so the trust boundary we study does not
exist. Denial of service through resource exhaustion sits in the taxonomy as
\meth{resource-abuse}. It is the thinnest cell we register: after the full-text
coding pass it holds \NMechResourceAbuse{} mechanism and
\NDefResourceAbuse{} defenses. We report it and draw no conclusions from a
cell that small.

\subsection{The safety regime}

There is no adversary, so this is not a threat model and we do not call it
one. Content goes wrong through ordinary pipeline behavior.
A retrieved document is stale. A passage is irrelevant but topically close.
Important text sits in the middle of a long input. A retrieved fact
contradicts what the model learned in training. The safety literature assumes this regime and evaluates on average-case benchmarks. That is the right
choice when nothing adapts.

\subsection{Why both, and what connects them}

The two regimes differ in exactly one assumption: adaptivity. They share the
channel, the position in the context window, and the downstream consequence.
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} shows that \PctOneRegime{}\% of published defenses
answer to one regime and not the other.

\textbf{This paper asks whether that single validation transfers.} The
assumption behind it is reasonable. A method that makes a model robust to a
stale document works on the same surface that an attacker writes to. Nobody
has simply tested it at scale. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests it.
