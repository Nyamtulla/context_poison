Two research communities study the same failure in large language model agents,
and neither tests the other's work. Security researchers study context that an
attacker changes on purpose, and publish attacks and defenses. Machine learning
researchers study context that becomes wrong on its own, and publish mechanisms
and fixes. The vocabularies differ; the failure does not. In both cases the
model cannot tell which part of its context deserves trust. We measure that
separation rather than assert it: from \NCorpus{} papers read in full we
recover \NPureTrackPairs{} confirmed (defense, attack) pairs, of which only
\NCrossCommunityPairs{} (\PctCrossCommunity{}\%) cross between the two
communities. One direction is empty outright: \textbf{no security defense in
our corpus has ever been evaluated against a machine learning failure
mechanism.}

So we ran that experiment. We executed \NCampaignPairs{} (defense, attack)
pairs across \NCampaignImpl{} implementations and \NCampaignEpisodes{} scored
episodes, each from its authors' released code and weights, under an
attack-works-first control and a fixed detectability threshold. At the dose
most favourable to a defense, not one of 15 (defense, payload) pairs shows a
resolvable benefit, and the reason is a single mechanism rather than a list of
failures. These methods arbitrate between trusting the context and trusting the
model's parameters, and moving that dial relocates probability mass without
creating any: suppress memory and the attacker receives what memory held,
suppress the attacker and memory receives it back. Across three defenses the
two movements cancel to within 7 points. Neither moves mass toward the real
document, because the attack is inside the context. The one defense that
resisted loses 29\% of its protection to an attacker who optimizes against the
single quantity its rule measures.
