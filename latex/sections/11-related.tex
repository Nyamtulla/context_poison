\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Systematizations of a single literature}

The security side has been systematized repeatedly and well. Work on the
agent attack surface enumerates attack classes against tool-using
systems~\cite{dehghantanha-2026-sok,chu-2026-systematic}, including the skill
and tool channels specifically~\cite{jiang-2026-sok}. Work on the
prompt-injection landscape codes injection papers into structured comparison
tables by method, surface, victim goal and
capability~\cite{yusifov-2026-taxonomy,duarte-2026-systematic}. Others take a
lifecycle or stack view of where an LLM system is
exposed~\cite{natanzi-2026-lifecycle,nemure-2026-llm}, trace long-term memory
as its own attack surface~\cite{lin-2026-survey}, or survey agent
trustworthiness broadly~\cite{qi-2026-trustworthy,mohan-2026-agent}. All are
careful. All are confined to the security literature.

The machine learning side has its own systematizations, and they do not
overlap. Knowledge conflict between retrieved text and parametric memory has
been surveyed on its own terms~\cite{xu-2024-knowledge}, as a problem of
accuracy rather than of adversaries. That survey and the security ones above
describe the same context window and share almost no references, which is the
separation this paper measures rather than asserts.

Three 2026 works sit closest to ours. A taxonomy-to-analysis study of the
prompt-injection threat landscape~\cite{wang-2026-landscape} codes injection
papers and builds a benchmark for agent behavior under context-dependent
interaction. PIArena~\cite{piarena-2026} is a modular platform for running
injection attacks against injection defenses, and reports that defenses once
judged effective lose robustness on more diverse attacks. A concurrent
evaluation of injection defenses~\cite{pidefense-eval-2026} finds that every
defense relying on the model to protect itself breaks under an adaptive
attacker, which is the same direction as our Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} result
and reached on a disjoint set of methods. All three are security-side, which
is what our corpus measures as the problem rather than a criticism of them.

Our contribution is not a better enumeration of either side. It is that one
search protocol, one screening pass and one taxonomy are applied to both, which
is what makes the distance between them measurable. A systematization drawn
from one literature cannot report that 9 papers in 10 do not cite the other, or
that \PctSameTrack{}\% of tested pairs stay inside one track, because the other track is
not in its corpus.

\subsection{Platforms that run attacks against defenses}

The closest precedent is not a survey but a platform.
DEEPSEC~\cite{ling-2019-deepsec} ran 16 adversarial-example attacks against
13 defenses in one harness, which is structurally what we do here. It was also
taken apart in public~\cite{carlini-2019-critique}. The criticisms are worth naming, because
they define what such a platform has to get right. DEEPSEC evaluated defenses
only against attacks crafted on undefended models. It reported robustness as an
average over attacks, not against the strongest. It reimplemented several
attacks and one defense incorrectly. And it reported no
variance at all.

We took those as a specification. We do not reimplement: every method runs
from its authors' released code and weights. The control-fires-first rule automates the
check Carlini had to run by hand, and it fired on FaithfulRAG, which we report
as not reportable rather than as defeated. We
report per-cell rather than averaging across threat models, and we say so where
a cross-payload comparison is confounded. We use greedy decoding throughout rather than
reporting error bars over a stochastic decoder, and we verified that choice by
rerunning a cell three times for byte-identical output
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}). The one criticism
we inherit is obliviousness, and Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}
and~\ref{sec:adaptive} state its direction and test the single claim that
needs it.

The reproducibility study of Olszewski et
al.~\cite{olszewski-2023-reproducibility} is the methodological precedent for
the other half: sampling papers from a venue, attempting to reproduce them, and
reporting what failed and why. Our roadblock accounting in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is in that genre, and our finding that several
published defenses cannot be run from their released artifacts is consistent
with theirs.

The second difference is that we execute. Existing systematizations in this
space report what papers claim their methods achieve. Section~\ref{sec:whoran}
is our reason for not doing that: in this field the reported outcome of a
(defense, attack) pair is close to determined by which side of the community
wrote the paper. A systematization that aggregates those reports inherits that
bias. Running the artifacts is the only way we found to avoid it.
