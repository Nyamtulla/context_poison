\section{Related work} \label{sec:related}

\subsection{Systematizations of a single literature}

Others have systematized the security side repeatedly and well. Work on the
agent attack surface enumerates attack classes against tool-using
systems~\cite{dehghantanha-2026-sok,chu-2026-systematic}, including the skill
and tool channels specifically~\cite{jiang-2026-sok}. Other work codes
injection papers into structured comparison tables, by method, by surface, by
victim goal and by
capability~\cite{yusifov-2026-taxonomy,duarte-2026-systematic}. Others take a
lifecycle or stack view of where an LLM system is
exposed~\cite{natanzi-2026-lifecycle,nemure-2026-llm}. Others trace long-term
memory as its own attack surface~\cite{lin-2026-survey}. Others survey agent
trustworthiness broadly~\cite{qi-2026-trustworthy,mohan-2026-agent}. All are
careful. All of them stay inside the security literature.

The safety side has its own systematizations, and they do not
overlap. One survey covers knowledge conflict between retrieved text and
parametric memory on its own terms~\cite{xu-2024-knowledge}. It treats the
problem as one of accuracy, not of adversaries. That survey and the security
ones above describe the same context window. They share almost no references, which
Section~\ref{sec:citation} measures.

Three 2026 works sit closest to ours. A taxonomy-to-analysis study of the
prompt-injection threat landscape~\cite{wang-2026-landscape} codes injection
papers and builds a benchmark for agent behavior under context-dependent
interaction. PIArena~\cite{piarena-2026} is a modular platform for running
injection attacks against injection defenses, and reports that defenses once
judged effective lose robustness on more diverse attacks. A concurrent
evaluation of injection defenses~\cite{pidefense-eval-2026} finds that every
defense which relies on the model to protect itself breaks under an adaptive
attacker. That is the same direction as our Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} result,
reached on a disjoint set of methods. All three stay on the security side. That is the gap this paper measures,
not a fault of theirs.

Our contribution is not a better enumeration of either side. It is that we
apply one search protocol, one screening pass and one taxonomy to both. That
is what makes the distance between them measurable. A systematization drawn from one literature cannot report that
\PctSecCitesSafety{}\% of security-regime papers cite a safety-regime one,
or that \PctSameRegime{}\% of tested pairs stay inside one regime. The other
literature is not in its corpus.

\subsection{Attack--defense evaluation platforms}

The closest precedent is not a survey but a platform.
DEEPSEC~\cite{ling-2019-deepsec} ran 16 adversarial-example attacks against 13
defenses in one harness, which is structurally what we do here. It was also
taken apart in public~\cite{carlini-2019-critique}. The criticisms are worth
naming, because they define what such a platform has to get right. DEEPSEC
evaluated defenses only against attacks crafted on undefended models. It
reported robustness as an average over attacks, not against the strongest. It
reimplemented several attacks and one defense incorrectly. And it reported no
variance at all.

We took those as a specification. We do not reimplement: every method runs
from its authors' released code and weights. The control-fires-first rule
automates the check Carlini had to run by hand. It fired on FaithfulRAG, which
we therefore report as not reportable rather than as defeated. We report per
cell and never average across regimes. We also say where a cross-payload
comparison is confounded. We use greedy decoding throughout, so we report no
error bars over a stochastic decoder. We verified that choice: a cell rerun
three times gives byte-identical output (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). The one
criticism we inherit is obliviousness, and Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}
and~\ref{sec:adaptive} state its direction and test the single claim that
needs it.

The reproducibility study of Olszewski et
al.~\cite{olszewski-2023-reproducibility} is the methodological precedent for
the other half. They sample papers from a venue, try to reproduce them, and
report what failed and why. Our roadblock accounting in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is in that genre. Several published defenses do
not run from their released artifacts, which agrees with what they found.

The difference from both precedents is that we execute. Existing
systematizations in this space report what papers claim their methods
achieve. Section~\ref{sec:whoran} shows why we do not. The side of the
community that wrote the paper almost determines the reported outcome of a
(defense, attack) pair. A systematization that aggregates those reports inherits that
bias. Running the artifacts is the only way we found to avoid it.
